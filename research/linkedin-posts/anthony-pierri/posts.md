## Post 1 — 2026-06-04
**Source:** https://www.linkedin.com/posts/anthonypierri_people-overcomplicate-positioning-there-activity-7468383258863423488-Phf2
**Why it matters:** Formato insignia — framework numerado (3 estrategias) con criterios 'choose this if' y CTA suave por DM. Plantilla de post educativo guardable.

> People overcomplicate positioning. There are really just three strategies... Strategy 1: Position in a mature category against existing vendors You place yourself in an established category and argue why you're better than the competition. The upside: Customers already have budget for it, actively search for it, and don't need educating. The risk: Mature categories have big, entrenched players. Without serious differentiation and resources, you'll struggle to make the shortlist. Choose this if... • the category is large enough to meet your growth goals • you have the resources to compete head-to-head for the whole market • OR you're willing to focus an underserved subsegment Strategy 2: Take an existing (but small) category to a new audience You position in a known (but likely nascent) category and sell it to people who haven't heard of it yet. (E.g. Skrillex bringing Dubstep to the mainstream. DocuSign didn't invent e-signature, but they focused on selling it to companies that used paper. Dropbox didn't invent file sharing, but they popularized it.) The upside: • Less direct competition, more greenfield opportunity The risk: • You have to educate the market rather than waiting for inbound demand • You also inherit the category's reputation (for good or bad) Choose this if... • you believe the category will grow and you can become a leader as it solidifies • you have the marketing patience to play the long game Strategy 3: Create your own category You refuse to fit into anyone else's box and seek to build a new one. The upside: You get full control to define both the problem and solution framing. The risks: • you need to pick the right-sized problem (too broad and it's unbelievable) • first movers rarely win (Airbnb beat Vrbo, Google beat AltaVista), and large incumbents may simply copy your blueprint once you've proven the market, as Microsoft did to Slack. Choose this if... • you genuinely believe any existing category label would hurt more than help • you're prepared to invest years building awareness from scratch If you need help with your positioning, shoot me a DM and mention this post.

## Post 3 — 2026-05-07
**Source:** https://www.linkedin.com/posts/anthonypierri_ai-companies-are-your-prospects-asking-activity-7458172886483189760-6kmU
**Why it matters:** Framework de messaging de dos opciones (why AI vs why YOUR AI) con ejemplos nombrados (Notion, Octonomy). Convierte un concepto de positioning en post estructurado.

> AI COMPANIES:Are your prospects asking "why should we use AI?"Or are they asking "why should we use YOUR AI?"There are two ways to message AI agent products:1) Assume your audience doesn't know what they are and isn't evaluating them, and you need to explain why they are worth looking intoThis makes sense in more immature ends of the market: legacy industries, highly regulated, slower moving industries, etc. There are still large portions of the enterprise that haven't jumped into the agent-adding frenzy. There are also individuals who are highly skeptical of AI as a concept (even if their only experience has been chatbots). They likely haven't gone deep into agents, and would need an explanation of what they are. This approach can be summed up in two words: WHY AI?If you're going after this more immature market segment, you need to answer WHY should someone adopt this new category they aren't familiar with? An example from Notion: "Notion agents capture knowledge, answer questions, and push projects forward — all while you sleep." This definition assumes someone doesn't know what AI agents are or how they work. This definition could apply to every other agent on the market. So it only makes sense for an audience that isn't aware of those other agents. But what if your audience DOES know about agents? Then you have approach number 2: 2) Assume your audience knows what agents are and is actively evaluating them or even using themThis other end of the market is more mature.They likely HAVE agents — or are on the verge of getting them. If you want to win this group, you don't have to explain WHY AI... they already know the why. Now you need to answer... why OUR ai? (I.e. what would make our AI different/better than the other ones being evaluated.) An example is Octonomy. They say: "The best AI platform for complex knowledge. 95% accuracy. No hallucinations." They are trying to show their differentiation against other AI agent vendors. Because they assume their audience IS mature, and knows the other agents exist. So they are trying to answer the question: "why should you choose OUR agents over the other ones you're evaluating."----------------If you're an AI company, think about your target market. Where are they in terms of adoption? And then evaluate your own homepage: Are you answering their core question?Or are you talking past them?

## Post 4 — 2026-05-04
**Source:** https://www.linkedin.com/posts/anthonypierri_your-product-managers-may-be-silently-destroying-activity-7457088895650910208-a6AL
**Why it matters:** POV contrarian que liga positioning con producto (4 riesgos de Marty Cagan). Hook fuerte + 'casi nadie ve esto': enseñar provocando.

> Your Product Managers may be silently destroying your ability to go-to-market effectively.Marty Cagan popularized the concept of four risks when creating a new product or feature: 1) value(will they buy it?)2) usability(can they use it?)3) feasibility(can we build it?)4) business viability(should we build it?)The fourth risk is often calculated... WITHOUT considering the impact on the product's existing positioning. Just because a MARKET for a feature exists, doesn't mean that your company can COMPETE in this market. So you can falsely come to the conclusion that a feature/feature-set passes the "viability" test, when in reality your business has no ability to win in that new market.But I find most product roadmap discussions miss this foundational truth all together! Nobody even realizes that supporting new use cases means entering completely new markets!Every use case has: → its own competitive alternatives→ its own required value framing→ its own demanded proof pointsJust because you CAN quickly spin up a new feature that solves a new use case... doesn't mean you SHOULD.And even though the product team SHOULD be highly connected to the GTM side of the business, it's just often not the case (especially in much larger orgs).
